# Three Moods in One Frame

Worked production brief for [Mood Design](mood-design.md). These are authored concepts, not rendered results or depicted book scenes. Adapt to the actual project; no scene, geographic location or solar orientation was supplied for this example.

## Fixed scene and comparison contract

Assume a two-storey home with pale mineral plaster, timber details, a glazed living room, a covered terrace, a table with four chairs, a path and restrained planting. The terrace sits on the right; the path enters from the lower left. Show enough floor under and beyond the canopy to read shelter.

Use one eye-level camera, initially about 1.6 m high, a horizontal 3:2 frame and a proposed 40 mm lens on a 36 by 24 mm full-frame sensor. Find the required coverage once, then lock camera position, orientation, projection and crop. These values are starting assumptions for this scene, not a universal camera rule. Do not add depth-of-field blur for these comparisons.

Keep architecture, furniture, planting, base finishes and output resolution fixed. Vary light, weather, occupancy and small narrative props. Rain changes exposed surface condition, not the specified base material. The intended first read is the terrace/living space; people explain its use.

These are coherent art-direction alternatives, not a controlled experiment isolating one variable.

## A. A quiet start to the day

**Situation and intention:** one resident takes a cup onto the terrace before work. Communicate calm readiness rather than vacancy.

**Light:** soft early-morning sky, with a restrained directional contribution only if plausible for the building orientation. Keep facade planes distinguishable without forcing dramatic graphic shadows. Avoid turning the living room into either a black hole or an overbright display.

**Palette:** subdued greens, pale plaster and recognizable timber. A slightly cooler overall illumination is optional; avoid a uniform blue cast. The cup can provide a small secondary accent.

**Activity:** one relaxed person under cover looks toward the garden. A cup and a slightly pulled-out chair support the action. Do not add unrelated domestic clutter.

**Attention:** path, terrace, person, then the connection to the living room. Avoid the strongest highlights on edge-of-frame vegetation.

**Checks:** does the space remain inviting with the figure hidden? Is the figure readable at delivery size? Does weak illumination suggest a closed or neglected property?

**Correction order:** clarify the occupied destination and pose first. Do not compensate for apparent vacancy by adding a crowd or excessive saturation.

## B. Conversation on the terrace

**Situation and intention:** three people talk late in the day. Communicate hospitality, usable scale and relaxed shared life.

**Light:** warm lateral daylight with softer sky fill. Retain information about people, furniture and circulation under the canopy. A local living-room light contribution is optional when justified by the selected moment and scene.

**Palette:** warmer illuminated surfaces against relatively neutral or cooler unlit areas. Preserve pale plaster and differences within the timber; do not apply a blanket orange treatment.

**Activity:** two people sit and a third stands by the table. Poses and gaze establish a coherent conversation. A few cups or dishes support it. Keep the main route and doorway usable.

**Attention:** terrace as a single light-and-activity group, interaction, then living room. Clothing should not create three competing focal points.

**Checks:** does interaction read without a caption? Does occupancy explain the real dimensions? Does grouping make the terrace look cramped? Does architecture remain the subject?

**Correction order:** adjust poses, spacing and clothing contrast before adding light. Do not enlarge approved architecture to accommodate a composition.

## C. Shelter during rain

**Situation and intention:** two residents remain comfortable under the canopy while rain wets the garden and path. Communicate protection and proximity to nature.

**Light:** soft overcast late-day illumination with restrained local terrace or living-room light if such sources belong to the scene. Make the occupied shelter readable without turning it into an overexposed island.

**Palette:** muted garden and stone colors with local warmth in the occupied zone. Establish differences through illumination and surface condition, not two uniform color masks.

**Weather and surfaces:** wet exposed path; a drier protected floor according to shelter, wind direction and prior conditions. Reflections should agree with material and surroundings. Put puddles only where surface levels allow them. Do not invent arbitrary runoff from construction details.

**Activity:** two seated people remain calm under cover. Avoid an exposed hurried figure unless discomfort is deliberately part of the story.

**Attention:** a restrained path reflection supports movement toward the terrace, then calm occupants and the living room. Droplet contrast must not dominate this sequence.

**Checks:** is shelter obvious? Do occupants look protected? Could water patterns suggest leaks or defects? Does the mood remain readable with visible rain streaks hidden?

**Correction order:** reconcile wet/dry regions and shelter illumination before adding more visible precipitation. An overlay cannot replace coherent weather evidence.

## Comparative review

| Variant | Architectural experience | Unwanted reading |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet morning | Privacy and connection to the garden | Vacancy or closure |
| Conversation | Comfortable shared use | Crowding or staged behavior |
| Rain | A usable protected terrace | Leaks, exposure or discomfort |

Use the same output dimensions and color pipeline. Finished variants may need different exposure and white balance; record those differences. During a diagnostic test, hold relevant other settings fixed instead.

Review without titles, in color and grayscale, at full and small sizes. Record first read, apparent action, spatial legibility and contradictory cues. If useful, ask another viewer to describe the situation without prompting. This is a qualitative review, not an objective emotion score.
