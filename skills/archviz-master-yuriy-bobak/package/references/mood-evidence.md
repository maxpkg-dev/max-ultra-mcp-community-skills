# Illustrated Evidence for Mood Design

Source keys and coverage: [evidence register](book-evidence-register.md). These are qualitative readings of illustrations and author explanations, not measured psychological effects. Apply with [Mood Design](mood-design.md).

### 1. Weather establishes conditions; relationships give them emotional meaning

**Evidence:** Djamila Knopf's environment variations, L 219 and 241 (printed 220 and 242).

The same house, ladder, bicycle and flowers remain recognizable across the overcast and early-morning versions. The overcast view preserves more local color; the early-morning version narrows the palette, darkens the setting and introduces a small stretching character. This is not a controlled lighting-only comparison, because the character and atmospheric treatment also change.

The accompanying text explicitly allows several readings of overcast light: straightforward presentation, heaviness, anticipation or mystery. It similarly associates early morning with both a fresh beginning and wakeful solitude. The useful lesson is the range of possible interpretations, not a dictionary equating blue with sadness.

**ArchViz translation:** define a situation before choosing weather: "a quiet courtyard before the first residents leave" gives a clearer target than "make it cinematic." Test whether readable paths, a believable activity and the degree of occupancy support that situation. A blue tint alone does not establish time, solitude or comfort.

**Boundary:** do not transfer the early-morning text's absolute claim of no bounce light into a renderer instruction. Keep artistic emphasis distinct from a literal physical model; this study establishes no GI settings.

### 2. Warmth needs a place and a reason

**Evidence:** L 251 (printed 252), sunset variation of the house.

The warm pink-orange treatment is tied to a familiar domestic setting and a child carrying a lantern. The author discusses transition, return, romance and drama as possibilities. The image's small-scale activity and recognizable home support a particular reading of the warm light; those meanings do not follow from hue alone.

**ArchViz translation:** for a welcoming evening entrance, make the destination understandable: a visible threshold, a plausible light source and a clear approach. Keep the person's direction consistent with arriving if that is the story. Compare the image with the figure hidden: the architectural invitation should still be legible.

**Failure mode:** making the entire frame orange can erase the distinction between destination and surroundings, conceal material identity and produce a color cast without a clear story. This is a diagnostic possibility, not an argument that warm palettes are inherently wrong.

### 3. Rain can communicate quiet continuity rather than crisis

**Evidence:** L 255 (printed 256), roof studies 39a-39b.

The comparisons show soft spray or haze close to the thatched roof. The text connects roof runoff, wet stones, reflections and restrained color. The author describes an appeal in softened distinctions and the peaceful continuity of rain.

**ArchViz translation:** construct the weather through consistent surface and environmental evidence: appropriate wet areas, plausible shelter, runoff and a coherent background. Then choose the human response. Someone sheltered under a canopy can suggest comfort; exposed hurried movement can suggest inconvenience. Those proposed readings are my interpretation, not a depicted architectural case from the book.

**Failure mode:** a rain overlay over otherwise dry materials and unchanged vegetation may read as an added effect. Conversely, maximum reflections everywhere can shift attention from the architecture to the effect. Do not add puddles or runoff patterns that contradict the modeled drainage or setting.

### 4. Preserve recognizable local colors within the mood

**Evidence:** GUWEIZ's Dinner, L 274-275 (printed 275-276), stages 14-15.

The author tries a stronger color treatment, notices unwanted blue in some areas, and returns to a restrained lighting palette before introducing local colors. Skin, the table and seating act as recognizable color references. The accompanying discussion treats warm and cool as relative relationships rather than a mandatory orange-blue pair.

**ArchViz translation:** choose which material colors must remain credible before applying an overall grade. Check timber, stone, fabric and any specified finish in the intended lighting. A restrained grade can leave space for differences among those materials while retaining atmosphere.

**Test:** compare the proposed treatment with the ungraded render at the same exposure and inspect the required finishes. State whether the change is intended illumination, material alteration or post-processing; do not silently substitute one for another.

### 5. Figure orientation can reverse the story

**Evidence:** C 219 (printed 84), figure 34 A-B.

Two simple silhouettes retain the house and person at the doorway. Turning the traveler changes the author's reading from returning home to leaving. The example works without elaborate rendering or color.

**ArchViz translation:** each prominent person should have an intelligible relation to the space: approaching, waiting, meeting, working or departing. Align body orientation, gaze and nearby objects with that action. Random asset placement may contradict a welcoming brief even when the models themselves look convincing.

**Test:** hide decorative detail and describe the scene in one sentence. If the action cannot be read at the intended delivery size, simplify or reposition it. Avoid relying on a tiny gesture that disappears in the final image.

### 6. Viewers can observe a scene or feel addressed by it

**Evidence:** C 197 and 203 (printed 76 and 78), figures 20-21 and 25.

A concealed threatening figure creates anticipation because the viewer sees a relationship that has not yet become an event. A small person surrounded by large enclosing trees suggests vulnerability. A separate sketch has a character looking and pointing directly toward the viewer, making that relationship more immediate.

**ArchViz translation:** decide whether the camera belongs to a passerby, arriving resident or detached observer. A reception figure looking toward the camera can make arrival explicit, while people absorbed in their activities can preserve an observational view. Foreground framing may imply shelter or obstructed access depending on what remains visible.

**Boundary:** do not transfer the examples' threat to a residential or hospitality brief by default. A low or enclosed view is not automatically threatening, and direct eye contact is not automatically welcoming. Gesture, expression, scale and context must agree.

### 7. Tonal separation can change the felt distance from an event

**Evidence:** C 254 (printed 97), Sea Serpent studies 05b-05e.

The silhouettes remain broadly stable while values change. A bright circular shape within a dark range reads as a moon. In another study the photographer and serpent are closer in value; the caption describes greater perceived proximity. Separating the dark photographer from the pale distant action instead suggests a safer observation point.

**Principle:** mood can change through inferred distance and involvement, even without changing the drawn outline. This illustrates an interpretation of pictorial space, not a claim that luminance determines real distance.

**ArchViz translation:** compare a sheltered foreground looking toward a luminous courtyard with a view whose foreground and courtyard belong to a more continuous tonal group. Ask whether the intended experience is protected observation or immediate participation. Preserve real depth cues and access information while making the comparison.

**Test:** first compare a few grayscale studies with geometry fixed. Then develop color and lighting for the selected spatial reading. Do not interpret every image with compressed values as intimate: fog, low contrast and ambiguity can produce other readings.

### 8. Atmosphere and spatial clarity can be traded deliberately

**Evidence:** Nathan Fowkes's Jungle Ruins, P 170-171 (printed 165-166), alternatives 38-39 and the final piece.

One variation increases color interest while reducing value contrast; the author calls it flatter and more decorative, not simply worse. Another uses an unusual cold illumination for a fantastical reading. In the final discussion, overly prominent foreground edges are restrained and small birds add life and movement.

**ArchViz translation:** a soft editorial image may intentionally prioritize quiet color relationships over dramatic depth. A design-review image may need clearer construction and separation. Choose the appropriate balance for the deliverable instead of ranking every image by maximum depth or drama.

**Boundary:** the author's description of an unnatural color combination is an artistic characterization, not proof that every comparable cool light is physically impossible. Do not label lighting physically impossible from warm/cool relationships alone.
